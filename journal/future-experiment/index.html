NOTES FROM THE GARDEN / 02

An Experiment in
Radical Listening

Letting listening and reflection shape the rules of composition.

BEGINNING SOON / LISTENINGERIC SOUTHER

I’m preparing a long-term experiment that begins with a collection of sounds recorded from my synthesizer and modular practice. I want to live with this material for years: transform it, compose with it, and keep returning to hear what else it can become. Listening Garden is the software I’m developing for that exploration. The garden begins with these recordings and grows through what happens to them.

The sonic corpus gives me a structure for exploring relationships. Alongside the recordings, I can gather measurements, processing histories and listening notes. A sound might become interesting through its resemblance to another, through a contrast, or through something I hear only after placing them together. Keeping the collection available lets me return to those encounters and try another direction.

I’m looking for moments of change that stand out. Something emerges, and I want to follow it: what can it say, how far can it stretch, what happens when it meets another process? This is close to how I work with video signals. I begin to find a vocabulary through engaging the material, then combine what I’ve found to see what else appears.

01 / THE INSTRUMENT

The instrument is part of the experiment

The software shapes how I encounter these sounds. I call its sound-processing modules FX organisms. Through processes such as delay, filtering and feedback, they act on the sounds moving through them and respond to modulation from elsewhere in the system. I’m continuing to develop their agentive capabilities, exploring how their behavior can become more varied and responsive over time.

Mycelium connects the organisms through modulation, much like control-voltage patching in a modular synthesizer. I think of it as patch cables growing between processes, carrying changes from one into another.

I’m interested in how individual organisms might develop different behaviors, remember their encounters and respond to the conditions around them. As the instrument develops, I want to hear what those capacities make possible in the music.

02 / FOLLOWING THE CHANGES

Following the changes

The Garden’s biological statistics give me ways to inspect the collection’s composition and activity. For this experiment, I also want to follow sonic evolution more closely. What remains recognizable after a sound has been transformed? Which qualities persist across its variations? When does a change open a relationship I hadn’t heard before?

Following those questions means keeping the sound, its transformations and my responses connected. The measurements offer one way to notice patterns across the collection. Returning to listen lets me explore what those patterns feel like and what I want to do with them.

03 / A LISTENING RECORD

A listening record

I’m preparing a listening process that brings together spoken annotations, marked moments and reflections after a piece ends. I want to preserve the first reaction alongside what I think later, including the moments when my response changes.

Through radical listening, I annotate each sound using my own vocabulary: Moss for ambient, Bloom for beautiful, Vine for surprise, and Roots for rhythmic. These words trace how I hear and respond to the sound, and give me something to return to as my listening changes.

Over one to three years, or longer, I want to use this record to develop the compositional system alongside my own listening. The goal is music composed through algorithms or open-weight models, with my recordings and responses helping shape the process. Can the Garden carry something from one encounter into the next? Can it remember a preference while continuing to surprise me? And how will my own taste change as I live with what we make?
