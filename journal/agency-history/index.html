When a composer makes a rule, where does the music happen? In the rule, in the material it acts on, in the performance, or in the decision to keep what comes back? I keep returning to this question while making Listening Garden. It has a much longer history than Artificial Intelligence.

01 / BEFORE COMPUTERS

A score can open a choice

In the early eleventh-century Micrologus, Guido of Arezzo described a way to connect the vowels of a text with pitches. Whether that chapter was chiefly for composing, teaching or improvising remains debated. Still, it offers a precise early case of a person preparing an input and a procedure helping determine the notes. [1]

Athanasius Kircher’s Arca musarithmica, described in 1650, used stored musical patterns and tables to set words for voices. A person operated it and still had choices to make. Later, Johann Philipp Kirnberger’s 1757 printed musical dice game let a throw select among prepared passages. The possible music was shaped before chance entered. These are different practices; neither is a fully autonomous composer. [2] [3]

02 / CHANCE AND CODE

What gets fixed, what gets released?

John Cage used I Ching chance operations while composing Music of Changes in 1951. The resulting piano score specifies the performance in detail: chance made decisions during composition, not afresh each time a pianist plays. [4]

Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson described a different arrangement in their 1958 paper on the Illiac Suite: a computer generated notes that were screened by programmed compositional rules, producing material for a string quartet. Human choices remained in defining those rules and realizing the result. [5]

These examples move decisions to different places: text, table, chance operation and program. Other works leave room for a system to unfold, or for people to decide while making and hearing it.

03 / OTHER OPENINGS

A system has more than one kind of opening

Iannis Xenakis used probability to organize whole populations of sounds: a composer could shape their density and distribution without specifying every relationship as a note-by-note line. His stochastic thinking began before his computer works. In Roland Kayn’s cybernetic music, interconnected electronic signals and feedback loops let a process unfold within a designed network. One case arranges probabilities; the other lets signals act on one another. [6] [7]

Other scores make room for people. Anthony Braxton’s 1988 account of his music system allows instrumental parts to move among works and lets performers negotiate tempo and dynamics. Pauline Oliveros’s Sonic Meditations turns instructions toward listening and sound-making together. Her practice is a comparison about attention and participation, not an example of a machine generating music. [8] [9]

Across visual art, Sol LeWitt’s “Doing Wall Drawings” gives another form of shared decisions: a plan is carried out by draftspeople, with interpretation inside its bounds. These are selected comparisons, not one continuous history or a claim that these artists influenced the Garden. [10]

04 / IN THE GARDEN

Listening can change the next rule

Artificial Intelligence adds another participant to my own process: it can help propose and revise rules. In Feral Current, I guided the exploration through artistic direction and listening, and the Garden’s audio engine performed the scores we developed. I heard what happened and could change the next request. That loop makes authorship feel less like one command and more like an exchange over time.

I am using these histories as comparisons, not claiming that the Garden descends directly from any one of them. The question I want to keep open is concrete: which decisions did I make, which did a procedure make, and what did listening lead me to change?

Hear some different openings

These recordings sit beside the historical questions above. Some are different works from those named in the text. Each link opens the label’s own listening page; no recording is copied here.

NOTES / SOURCES

Follow the sources

  1. Guido of Arezzo, Micrologus, chapter XVII (early eleventh century); Lucy Green, “What is Chapter 17 of Guido’s Micrologus about?” (2007), on competing readings of the passage.
  2. Athanasius Kircher, Musurgia universalis, vol. 2, book VIII (1650), ETH-Bibliothek Zürich, Rar 610; interpreted here through Andrew A. Cashner, “Athanasius Kircher’s Arca musarithmica (1650) as a Computational System” (2024). Cashner distinguishes the human-operated historical apparatus from his software reconstruction.
  3. Johann Philipp Kirnberger, Der allezeit fertige Polonoisen- und Menuettencomponist (Berlin, 1757), digitized original, Library of Congress; Stefan Jena’s “Würfelmusik” explains the selection method.
  4. John Cage Trust, Music of Changes work record, including its composition date and the distinction between chance in composition and a fixed performance score.
  5. Lejaren A. Hiller Jr. and Leonard M. Isaacson, “Musical Composition with a High-Speed Digital Computer”, Journal of the Audio Engineering Society 6, no. 3 (1958): 154–160.
  6. Iannis Xenakis, Formalized Music, expanded edition (1992), chapters I and V; Benoît Gibson’s essay on stochastic music for the Xenakis Association distinguishes the early works and their distributions.
  7. Roland Kayn, “Cybernetic Music” and other artist writings, official Kayn site. His account describes control loops and feedback; this is not evidence that Listening Garden uses the same circuitry.
  8. Anthony Braxton, “Introduction to Catalog of Works” (1988), postulates and clarifications on autonomous parts, recombination, tempo and dynamics.
  9. Pauline Oliveros, Sonic Meditations, official publication listing; her 2012 CV identifies the scores. The complete collection has not been closely read for this short note.
  10. Sol LeWitt, “Doing Wall Drawings” (1971), reproduced in the Museum of Modern Art catalogue, printed p. 169. This is a visual-art comparison about written plans and human realization.
