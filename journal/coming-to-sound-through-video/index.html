Coming to soundthrough video

Making a tool, perceiving its behavior, and keeping the trail of revision

My way into algorithmic composition comes through video processing and research into artists developing their own systems from the 1970s onward. Listening Garden carries that experience into sound. I am interested in the relationship between making a tool and discovering what it allows me to perceive. A change opens a possibility; seeing or hearing its result gives me something to respond to. That response can become another gesture, a different rule, or a revision to the instrument.

An image being made

In Video: The Reflexive Medium, Yvonne Spielmann locates reflexivity in video's technical character. Its image is continuously constructed and reconstructed through signals. The medium exists as a changing process, even when a particular use conceals that character. Feedback and closed circuit are especially revealing forms of this ongoing transformation. [1]

Spielmann’s discussion of Violin Power describes image-forming processes that can be observed and modified in real time through a musical instrument. Performing involves attending to processes while acting within them. [2]

Spielmann's account of Woody Vasulka's Vocabulary and Artifacts also places setup and control alongside discovery. The apparatus makes phenomena available that the artist can investigate. [3] I recognize a useful question here: how does a tool let me encounter something I could not fully specify beforehand?

Marita Sturken’s 1996 essay about Steina and Woody Vasulka offers a useful description of this encounter: “not masters of a tool but interpreters of its capabilities.” Her account does not remove the artist from the system. Later in the essay, the autonomy she describes remains qualified by human intervention. I recognize a tension here: building an instrument gives me ways to act, but attending to what it can do may change what I intended to make. [5]

Different ways of returning

An electronic signal returning through a circuit and my attention returning to a result operate differently. In one, a signal is transformed through a technical path. In the other, I notice something, judge it, and decide what to change. Those activities can meet in practice. Keeping their differences audible helps me ask where a decision is happening.

Crutchfield’s study of video feedback gives this return a concrete form: an image becomes part of the conditions that produce the next image. What returns is not simply repeated; it helps change what happens next. [4]

Video feedback is a generative loop: output returns as input, changing the next pass, and the resulting image offers a way to encounter the process and intervene through the controls. [4] Steina’s performance and Spielmann’s account help frame a proposition I want to test in the Garden: listening and reflection as ways to encounter and reshape its compositional processes.

Spielmann's reflexivity is broader than either a visible control panel or an artist thinking about their work. Her account prompts a question I can bring to the Garden: how can its compositional behavior become perceptible through its results? If a relationship between sounds surprises me, what can I learn about the conditions that produced it? What would I change to hear that relationship differently?

Keeping the revisions

Over six months, soundmaking and tool development have accumulated together in Listening Garden. I think of that activity as sedimentation: sounds, rules, experiments, and revisions forming layers through use. With an Artificial Intelligence collaborator, I can return to the code and change the instrument. My listening and reflection help determine what to hear again, keep, or revise.

I want the journal to retain the messy trail of those decisions. A polished result can leave out the hesitations and changes that made it possible. Recording how my understanding shifts matters alongside using that understanding to steer the next piece. The layers remain part of the work, even when a later version takes another direction.

The sustained experiment connecting systematic annotation with future composition is still ahead. I want to investigate what that record could contribute without deciding its outcome in advance. For now, these notes can make the relation between making and listening visible: what I changed, what I noticed, and what remains unresolved.
